Feynman diagrams—those squiggly lines you see on physicists’ chalkboards—are a code and a language. 

As a code, they are a mathematical shorthand for the multidimensional calculus of quantum field theory. As a language, they let physicists tell the story of the subatomic dance of particles that gird our universe. 

To learn to use them as a code, one must master several years’ worth of graduate-level mathematics and physics. However, as a language for story telling, the diagrams themselves are a powerful tool to share how we presently understand the most fundamental laws of nature. 

Every culture in human history has had a cosmology: a story for how we came to be and how the universe works. Contemporary science has its own cosmology rooted in fundamental physics and ever more precise experimental observation. Unlike the past cultures that built humankind, however, our cosmology’s triumphs come at the cost of becoming more esoteric—knowledge where we tell each other that one must toil through graduate school in order to access this knowledge. 

One consequence of this has been that we have lost the need to tell each other the stories of our shared universe. We’ve gotten lost in long equations and technical terms that keep physicists and non-physicists separated. By turning to simple diagrams and pictures, we can go back to humanity's storytelling roots. Feyntasia is a blog, a graphic novel, and a course on particle physics. 
Like the quantum reality that Feynman diagrams represent, it is all of these things at once. Most of all, it is a story of how our universe works. 

Over the next several posts you will learn what a Feynman diagram is, what they represent, how we go from these lines and squiggles to particle physics, and hopefully, gain a deeper understanding of our cosmology.
